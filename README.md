## Artefact 2: AI Technology Detects a Koala Crossing in Real Time

[![AI-powered camera detecting a koala crossing](./artefact-2-thumbnail.png)](https://news.griffith.edu.au/2026/03/03/ai-technology-detects-real-time-koala-crossing-in-first-for-field/)

https://news.griffith.edu.au/2026/03/03/ai-technology-detects-real-time-koala-crossing-in-first-for-field/

### Summary of the artefact

In this article, Rosengreen (2026) describes how a prototype AI-powered camera embedded in a smart road sign successfully detected and recorded a koala crossing the road in real time. The camera employs edge computing (a decentralised IT architecture that processes data near its source) and real-time video analysis, which may, in the future, save countless Australian wild animals' lives!

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

I chose this article because it expands on the workshop's discussion of AI-enabled cameras like Waymo through a different lens. Moreover, such a technology somewhat mitigates AI’s negative ecological impact by helping to protect Australian wildlife. I believe this technology can be adapted for other animals in the future. Hopefully, one day we will not see the typical Australian roadway landscape filled with downed kangaroos and possums.

## Artefact 3: Scholarly Article

[![Aeneas AI system for contextualising ancient Latin inscriptions](./artefact-3-thumbnail.png)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09292-5)
*Source: Assael et al. (2025, p. 141).*

https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09292-5

### Summary of the artefact

The article by Assael et al. (2025, p. 141) introduces Aeneas, a generative neural network for contextualising ancient texts, which is not limited by exact text matching, as it uses "historically enriched embeddings". Aeneas simultaneously solves three problems: text restoration, geographic attribution, and chronological attribution. The model can also process images of inscriptions in addition to text transcription, which significantly increases the accuracy of geographic identification, making it recognised as useful by 23 expert historians in the study in 90% of cases!

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

I chose this article because I've dreamed of becoming an IT archaeologist since childhood, and AI makes it possible! Using artificial intelligence like this opens up endless possibilities of adapting the technology for other ancient languages apart from Latin epigraphy to resolve unsolved mysteries of the past.

### Summary of the artefact: My Personal Reflection

The main lesson learned from the workshop's theory was the classification of AI, which is divided into three types: ANI (narrow AI, which exists today), AGI (general AI, capable of planning and making decisions like a human), and ASI (superAI, which will surpass humanity). I hadn't come across any specific specifications before. It was also interesting to hear from other class members how the older generation and vulnerable groups with mental health issues can perceive AI as thinking, feeling, and empathising, although all it can do is mimic understanding and feelings, as I come from a tech family where everyone thinks of it as an unspoken truth.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact

The screenshot captures two moments simultaneously: my chat message about the leaked police body-camera videos on YouTube, which could be misused unethically in the context of AI, and the workshop’s end as proof of attendance. I included this message in chat because AI training and content generation relying on illegally obtained or unlicensed data keep me up at night. This is an incredibly complex legal area that still lacks clear guidelines. Unlike standard copyright, where copying is easier to identify, tracing AI training data is hard since datasets and model processes are private. Content generated from illegal images is even worse. Thus, the South Australian case discussed earlier shows a teenager pleaded guilty to creating and sharing sexually explicit deepfakes and using an online service to distribute them, making illegal AI content part of modern life (Blandis 2026). Similar content can be created locally, distributed in private chats or via disappearing messages, and leave no obvious traces of the source data and tools used. Without access to devices, accounts, and the internal logs of an AI service, proving the origin of an image and establishing responsibility can be nearly impossible. This workshop once again made me think about what AI is capable of creating and if there’s an ethical way to use it without a legal framework that violates freedom of speech through limitations of any kind.
